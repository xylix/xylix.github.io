## People keep being surprised about the EA Slytherins

I'll try to point at the confusion(s). First confusion:
### But Slytherins don't care for altruistic goals!

Bear with me for a lens about what kind of people the houses are _selecting for_.(Of course all houses have significant sets of virtues from both buckets.)

I think we can divide the Hogwarts Houses into _methods houses_:
- Slytherin values social shrewdness, understanding the status game, 
- Ravenclaw values well-crunched analysis. Intellectualism. Academicness.

and _value houses_:
- Gryffindor values courage and doing what's right
- Hufflepuff values kindness and loyalty


And this also leads naturally into an explanation for why Slytherins would want to do EA: Slytherins have a pretty normal amount of variance in their values, accounting for their backgrounds etc. (Observing the literal House Slytherin: Being born into money or aristocracy does tend one to desire social shrewdness.)

Aside: Why do even the non-reprehensible Slytherin's easily get called evil? I think it's significantly because a significant part of the population considers the Slytherin-virtuous _methods_ reprehensible; manipulation of social realities, truth, understanding that what people say and what they think are different layers, is considered something that you're not supposed to look at.

## Claude ideas:

- The "when is cunning licensed" question generalizes into a taxonomy. War = yes, business = squirmy, academia = pretend no but actually yes, dating = officially no but everyone knows, politics = obviously yes, charity = emphatically no. The pattern: cunning gets licensed when the domain is culturally admitted to be adversarial, and charity's self-image as non-adversarial is precisely why EA-Slytherin reads as oxymoronic. That's a whole section and it writes itself.
- Quirrell is the crux compressed. He's doing explicit utilitarian calculation with Slytherin methods and half the fandom thinks he's right. Worth more than one line — he's the thought experiment your reader already has cached.

- Historical examples:
    - Suntzi and the Art of War 
        - It's basically a Slytherin playbook
        > Appear weak when you are strong, and strong when you are weak.
        - Guess: And it's probably easier for people to stomach as a handbook about war, than as a literal business handbook. But it also happens to be a good business handbook. Who would have thought manipulating expectations and the information-environment would be a winning move in an adversially competitive field?
        - Claude:
            > The reason it's stomachable as war and squirmy as business is that we've culturally agreed war is a domain where Gryffindor virtue gets people killed, so cunning earns a pass. Business we pretend is meritocratic. But any adversarial-under-uncertainty domain rewards the same skills: negotiation, hiring, academic politics, dating, parenting teenagers. The discomfort is the tell — we've mislabeled which domains are adversarial.
    - Odysseus
        - Claude:
            > Odysseus. The Greeks themselves couldn't decide if he was a hero or a moral stain, and that ambivalence is the Slytherin question in its oldest form. Dante puts him in hell for the Trojan Horse; Homer makes him the protagonist. Same methods, same goals (get home, protect crew, win war), opposite verdicts — and the verdict tracks the reader's priors about when cunning is allowed.
